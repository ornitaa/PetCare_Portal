Emergency Care Guide

Quick first-aid guidance for common pet emergencies while arranging professional veterinary care.

First response

1
Stay safe

Check the surroundings before approaching. An injured or frightened pet may bite or scratch.

2
Check the pet

Look for breathing difficulty, severe bleeding, collapse, seizures, major injuries, or poisoning.

3
Minimize movement

Keep the pet calm and avoid unnecessary movement, especially after falls, accidents, or suspected fractures.

4
Contact a veterinarian

Arrange professional care as soon as possible and transport the pet carefully.

Urgent

Choking or Difficulty Breathing

What you may notice

  • Struggling or noisy breathing
  • Gagging or repeated coughing
  • Pawing at the mouth
  • Blue, grey or unusually pale gums
  • Collapse or loss of consciousness

What to do

  • Keep the pet as calm as possible.
  • If an object is clearly visible in the mouth and can be removed safely, carefully remove it.
  • Do not blindly push your fingers deep into the mouth or throat.
  • Seek emergency veterinary care immediately if breathing remains difficult.
Urgent

Severe Bleeding

What to do

  • Place clean gauze, a clean towel, or clean cloth over the wound.
  • Apply steady, firm pressure.
  • If blood soaks through, add more material on top instead of repeatedly removing the original layer.
  • Keep the pet quiet and arrange urgent veterinary care.

Avoid

  • Repeatedly checking underneath the dressing.
  • Applying unknown powders or household remedies.
  • Allowing the pet to run or walk unnecessarily.
Emergency

Suspected Poisoning

What to do

  • Move your pet away from the suspected substance if it is safe to do so.
  • Keep the product container, label, medication packet, plant sample, or other identifying information.
  • Contact a veterinarian immediately.
  • Tell the veterinarian what the substance was, approximately how much may have been involved, and when the exposure happened.

Do not

  • Do not induce vomiting unless a veterinarian specifically instructs you to.
  • Do not give medicines or home remedies unless directed by a veterinary professional.
  • Do not wait for symptoms if you know a potentially dangerous substance was swallowed.
Protect from injury

Seizure

What to do

  • Move objects away that could injure the pet.
  • Keep the area quiet and reduce stimulation.
  • Note how long the seizure lasts.
  • Contact a veterinarian after the seizure and seek urgent care for prolonged or repeated seizures.

Do not

  • Do not restrain the pet forcefully.
  • Do not place your hands or any object inside the pet's mouth.
  • Keep your face and hands away from the mouth during and immediately after the seizure.
Emergency

Heatstroke

Possible signs

  • Heavy or excessive panting
  • Drooling
  • Weakness or wobbliness
  • Difficulty breathing
  • Vomiting or diarrhea
  • Collapse or seizures

What to do

  • Move the pet out of direct heat immediately.
  • Begin cooling with cool water.
  • Use airflow from a fan if available.
  • Avoid ice or extremely cold water.
  • Arrange veterinary treatment immediately, even if the pet appears to improve.
Veterinary assessment

Burns

What to do

  • Remove the pet from the source of the burn if it is safe.
  • For a thermal burn, gently cool the affected area with cool water.
  • Keep the area clean and prevent licking if possible.
  • Contact a veterinarian, particularly for extensive, deep, electrical, or chemical burns.

Avoid

  • Ice directly on damaged skin.
  • Butter, oils, creams, or household remedies.
  • Breaking blisters.
Urgent

Fracture or Serious Trauma

What to do

  • Keep the pet as still and calm as possible.
  • Support the body when moving the pet.
  • A firm board, carrier base, or other stable surface may help during transport.
  • Seek veterinary care immediately after serious falls, vehicle accidents, or major trauma.

Do not

  • Do not attempt to straighten a suspected broken bone.
  • Do not force the pet to stand or walk.
  • Avoid twisting the neck or spine during transport.
Emergency

Collapse or Unconsciousness

What to do

  • Check whether the pet appears to be breathing.
  • Keep the airway area clear and avoid unnecessary movement.
  • Transport the pet carefully, supporting the head, neck, and body.
  • Seek emergency veterinary attention immediately.

Important

Sudden collapse or unconsciousness should always be treated as a serious medical emergency.

Safe transport

Getting your pet to veterinary care

Injured pets may be frightened or painful. Handle them slowly and avoid unnecessary movement.

Use a carrier for smaller pets when possible. Support the head and body after major trauma. Keep the environment quiet during transport. Call the clinic ahead when possible.
